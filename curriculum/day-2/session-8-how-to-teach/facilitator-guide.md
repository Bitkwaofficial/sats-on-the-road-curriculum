# Session 8, How to teach this

| | |
|---|---|
| **Duration** | 60 minutes |
| **Audience** | Day 2, future ambassadors (people who will teach others and onboard merchants) |
| **Source** | New material for Sats On The Road. Builds on the teaching the ambassadors saw across Day 1 |

## Objectives

By the end of this session, each ambassador can:

1. Describe how adults learn best, and plan a session around doing, not just listening.
2. Lay out a two-hour community session with a clear start, middle, and end, and keep it on time.
3. Use simple techniques to keep a room awake and taking part.
4. Answer the most common Bitcoin questions and myths calmly and honestly.
5. Name the mistakes new facilitators make, and how to avoid them.

## Materials

- Projector for `slides.md`, or just a flip chart if there is no screen.
- A timer or phone clock everyone can see.
- Printed copies of the Day 1 one-session plan for reference.
- Slips of paper with common questions and myths written on them (see "Common questions").
- Space for pairs to spread out and practise.

## Before you start

The ambassadors have now sat through two days as learners. This session flips them to the front of the room. The single most useful thing you can give them is confidence that they already know enough to teach a beginner, because they were a beginner two days ago. Keep it practical. The heart of the hour is the practice-teach, not the talk.

## Minute-by-minute plan

### 0–5 min, You are ready sooner than you think (5)
Open with the main message: you do not need to be an expert to teach a beginner. You need to be one step ahead and honest about the rest. Share the plan for the hour and point out that most of it is them practising, not you talking.

### 5–17 min, How adults learn (12)
Cover the basics:
- Adults learn by doing, then understanding, not the other way round. Hands on the phone first.
- They learn what solves a real problem they have, so connect every idea to money they actually use.
- They switch off when talked down to. Respect the room; many have run businesses for years.
- People remember little from a lecture and a lot from one thing they did themselves.
- Repetition in different forms (say it, show it, let them do it) beats saying it louder.

### 17–27 min, Anatomy of a two-hour session, and time (10)
Walk the shape of the short one-session class:
- **Open (10 min):** welcome, why money matters here, what they will leave with.
- **Teach and do (85 min):** money and why ours fails, what Bitcoin is, set up a wallet, one real Lightning payment.
- **Close (15 min):** questions, where to get help, how to keep going.
- **Buffer (10 min):** things always run long.
Time management rules: start on time even with a small crowd, keep one eye on the clock, cut depth not the hands-on part, and always protect time for the real payment at the end.

### 27–35 min, Keeping the room engaged (8)
Simple, low-tech techniques:
- Ask questions instead of only giving answers. Wait for replies.
- Use local examples, local prices, local names.
- Get phones in hands early and often.
- Pair people up so the shy ones are helped by a neighbour, not put on the spot.
- Move around the room. Use names. Celebrate the first successful payment out loud.

### 35–55 min, Practice-teach in pairs (20)
Run the exercise (see `exercise.md`). Each person teaches a five-minute segment to their partner, who plays an interested but unsure beginner, then they swap and give each other one kind, specific piece of feedback. Circulate and note strong moments to share in the debrief.

### 55–60 min, Debrief and mistakes to avoid (5)
Quick round: one thing that went well, one thing to try next time. Close with the common mistakes list: talking too long, using jargon, skipping the hands-on part, pretending to know an answer, and running out of time before the real payment.

**Total: 60 minutes.**

## Talking points

- The best teacher in the room is the person who learned it most recently. That is you.
- Teach to the slowest learner. If the most unsure person gets it, everyone got it.
- "I don't know, let's find out together" builds more trust than a confident wrong answer.
- A session is a success when people leave having done something, not having heard something.
- Your job is not to sound clever. It is to make Bitcoin feel ordinary and doable.

## Common questions

Use these as practice prompts and prepare honest, short answers. Hand them out on slips during the practice-teach.

**"Is Bitcoin a scam?"**
Bitcoin itself is open software anyone can check, with no owner. Scams use Bitcoin's name, the same way scams use bank names. Teach people to spot the scam, not to fear the tool.

**"Is it legal here?"**
`[OWNER TO FILL: legal status per country]`. Give the honest local answer and never guess. If unsure, say you will check and point them to the country lead.

**"Will I get rich?"**
No promises. Bitcoin is a way to save and to send money, not a get-rich scheme. Its price can fall. Never predict a price.

**"It uses too much electricity, no?"**
Mining uses energy to keep the network secure, the way banks and gold mining use energy too. Keep the answer calm and short; do not get pulled into a debate that loses the room.

**"I am not good with technology."**
If you can send a photo on a phone, you can do this. We will go step by step and you will do it yourself today.

**"What if I lose my phone?"**
That is exactly why we back up the seed phrase. With the backup, your coins come back on a new phone.

**"What if someone asks something I cannot answer?"**
Say so honestly, write the question down, and bring the answer to the next session or ask the country lead. Trust grows from honesty, not from bluffing.
